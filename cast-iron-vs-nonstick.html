Walk into any kitchen store and you'll face the same fork in the road: a heavy black cast iron skillet, often somewhere in the $20–40 range, or a slick nonstick pan that might cost anywhere from $20 to $150. Both camps have loud fans. Having cooked on both nearly every day for years, here's the honest breakdown — and a clear answer on which to buy first.

Where cast iron wins

Heat retention. Cast iron holds heat like nothing else in your kitchen. That's why it puts a genuine crust on a steak, keeps its temperature when you drop in cold chicken thighs, and turns out cornbread with crisp golden edges. Thin nonstick pans lose heat the moment food hits them.

Durability. A cast iron skillet is a lifetime purchase — arguably a multi-generation one. You can use metal spatulas, run it screaming hot, slide it under the broiler, or set it over a campfire. There is no coating to scratch and no handle to melt.

It gets better with use. Every time you cook with fat, the seasoning (a layer of polymerized oil) builds. A well-used cast iron pan becomes naturally slick — not Teflon-slick, but close enough for fried eggs with a little butter.

Where nonstick wins

Delicate foods, zero drama. Eggs, crepes, fish fillets, and pancakes release effortlessly, even with minimal oil. If your weekday breakfast is scrambled eggs, nonstick is simply less stressful at 7 a.m.

Cleanup. A wipe with a soapy sponge and you're done. Cast iron cleanup is genuinely easy once you know the routine, but nonstick asks nothing of you at all.

Weight. A 10-inch cast iron skillet weighs about 5 pounds. If wrist strain is a concern, that matters every single day.

The real differences people gloss over

  • Nonstick is consumable. Even babied, the coating degrades. Plan on replacing a nonstick pan every 3–5 years — which changes the value math on expensive ones.
  • Cast iron has a learning curve, not a maintenance burden. Rinse it warm, dry it on the burner, wipe a drop of oil in. That's the whole ritual, and it takes 90 seconds.
  • Acidic foods. Long-simmered tomato sauce can strip young cast iron seasoning and pick up a metallic note. Keep a stainless or nonstick pan around for that.
  • High heat kills nonstick. Empty preheating or searing above medium-high shortens coating life fast. Cast iron is where your hard sears belong.

So which should you buy first?

Buy the cast iron first. It covers more territory — searing, roasting, baking, frying — costs less, and never needs replacing. Then add an inexpensive nonstick pan as your dedicated egg-and-fish pan, and treat it gently. That two-pan setup handles 95% of home cooking and typically lands well under $75 all in.
